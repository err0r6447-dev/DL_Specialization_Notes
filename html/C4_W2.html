Week 2 — Case Studies: Classic CNN Architectures

Course 4 (Convolutional Neural Networks) · Deep Learning Specialization · Andrew Ng

🔵 What This Week Is About

This week studies the CNNs that shaped the field. We trace the evolution from LeNet‑5 (1998, 60K params) through AlexNet (2012, 60M params) and VGG (2014) to ResNet (2015, with skip connections) and the Inception network (parallel branches). We then study efficiency-driven architectures — MobileNet and EfficientNet — and finish with practical advice: transfer learning, data augmentation, and benchmark tricks.

🟢 Big-Picture Narrative

Each architecture introduced one new idea that stuck:

  • LeNet‑5 → CNNs work for real tasks (digit recognition).
  • AlexNet → Deep + ReLU + GPU + big data = step change.
  • VGG → Simplicity: stacks of 3×3 convs.
  • ResNet → Skip connections let you go very deep (152 layers).
  • Inception → Multi-scale parallel filters + 1×1 bottleneck.
  • MobileNet → Depthwise separable convs for mobile.
  • EfficientNet → Jointly scale depth/width/resolution.

1. Why Look at Case Studies?

By now you know the basic building blocks: convolution, pooling, padding, stride, ReLU, softmax. But how do you combine them into something that actually works? The best way is to study networks that have worked — their designs are the outcome of enormous trial and error, and the intuitions they encode transfer directly to new tasks.

🟢 Analogy — Learning from Masterpieces

Just as an aspiring architect studies famous buildings before designing their own, an ML engineer studies famous networks. You learn common motifs (stacked 3×3 convs, residual blocks, bottleneck layers) that you can reuse.

Concretely, case studies teach you:

  • Reusable patterns — 3×3 stacking, residual blocks, 1×1 bottlenecks.
  • Design trade-offs — depth vs. width, accuracy vs. compute.
  • Practical numbers — what parameter counts, memory, and compute look like.
  • Transfer learning — pretrained backbones you can fine-tune on your own task.

2. LeNet‑5 (1998)

LeCun et al., 1998, "Gradient-based learning applied to document recognition." Designed for digit/zip-code recognition.

Figure 2.1 — LeNet‑5 architecture. Input 32×32×1 → CONV → POOL → CONV → POOL → FC → FC → softmax. Notice the pattern: \(n_H, n_W\) decrease, \(n_C\) increases.

🟡 Important — The Shape Shrink/Grow Pattern

As you go deeper into a CNN:

  • \(n_H\) and \(n_W\) (spatial size) decrease — pooling and/or stride shrink the grid.
  • \(n_C\) (channel depth) increases — later layers detect more abstract features.

This pattern appears in every modern CNN. LeNet‑5 was the first to establish it.

🔴 Warning — Two Modernisations

Modern implementations of LeNet typically use max pooling (not average) and ReLU (not sigmoid/tanh) in the hidden layers. These two changes alone give a noticeable accuracy boost — a lesson first popularised by AlexNet.

3. AlexNet (2012)

Krizhevsky et al., 2012, "ImageNet classification with deep convolutional neural networks." Won the ImageNet competition by a huge margin and kicked off the modern deep-learning era.

Figure 3.1 — AlexNet. Similar to LeNet but much larger: 227×227 input, 8 learnable layers, 60M parameters, ReLU activations, and max pooling.

What made AlexNet special

🟢 Intuition — Bigger + Faster + More Data

AlexNet didn't invent the CNN (LeNet‑5 did). What it did was combine depth + fast activations (ReLU) + GPU compute + ImageNet-scale data — and the result was a massive jump in accuracy. It proved that deep learning scales.

🔴 Warning — LRN Didn't Survive

Local Response Normalization appears in AlexNet but almost every modern network replaced it with batch normalization (from Course 2). Don't waste time implementing LRN — know it exists, move on.

4. VGG‑16 / VGG‑19 (2014)

📎 Additional Context

The provided slide for VGG (page 6) is corrupted in the PDF extract. The notes below cover the standard VGG design as it appears in the Deep Learning Specialization lectures, so you have complete coverage of the outlined "classic networks."

Simonyan & Zisserman, 2014. VGG's key insight is radical simplicity: instead of using a variety of filter sizes (11×11, 5×5, 3×3) like AlexNet, use only 3×3 convolutions stacked in depth.

🟢 Intuition — Why Stack Small Filters?

Two 3×3 convs have a receptive field of 5×5. Three 3×3 convs have a receptive field of 7×7. So a stack of small filters can see the same region as one large filter — but uses fewer parameters and adds more non-linearity (a ReLU after each conv).

VGG‑16 architecture highlights

  • Input: 224×224×3.
  • All convs are 3×3, stride 1, "same" padding.
  • All max pools are 2×2, stride 2.
  • Pattern: [CONV 3×3 ×2] → POOL → [CONV ×2] → POOL → [CONV ×3] → POOL → [CONV ×3] → POOL → [CONV ×3] → POOL → FC → FC → softmax.
  • ≈138M parameters — mostly in the FC layers.
  • VGG‑19 = same idea, 3 extra conv layers.
🟡 Important — VGG's Legacy

VGG's uniform design (always 3×3) made CNNs easy to reason about and easy to reimplement. Its architecture is still widely used as a pretrained backbone for transfer learning, especially in style transfer and perceptual-loss applications.

🔴 Warning — VGG Is Expensive

138M parameters and huge compute. Training VGG from scratch is slow; it's usually used pretrained. For new work, ResNet/EfficientNet are typically better defaults.

5. ResNet — Residual Networks (2015)

He et al., 2015, "Deep residual networks for image recognition." Won ImageNet 2015 and enabled networks with 152 layers (vs. ~20 for VGG).

5.1 The problem ResNet solves

Intuitively, a deeper network should be at least as good as a shallower one — you could always pad extra layers with identity mappings. But in practice, plain deep networks get worse training error as depth increases past a point. This is called the degradation problem.

Figure 5.1 — Left: In a plain network, training error eventually increases with depth (blue), contradicting theory (green). Right: ResNet keeps training error decreasing, enabling very deep networks.

5.2 The residual block

🔵 Definition — Residual Block

A residual block is a small sub-network where the input \(a^{[l]}\) is added directly to the output of a few layers ahead, via a skip connection (also called a shortcut).

$$ a^{[l+2]} = g\!\left(z^{[l+2]} + a^{[l]}\right). $$
Figure 5.2 — A residual block. The input \(a^{[l]}\) travels along the main path through two weight layers, and also along a shortcut directly to the addition node. The output is \(a^{[l+2]} = g(z^{[l+2]} + a^{[l]})\).

5.3 The math of a residual block

Plain path (two layers)
$$ z^{[l+1]} = W^{[l+1]} a^{[l]} + b^{[l+1]} \qquad a^{[l+1]} = g\!\left(z^{[l+1]}\right) $$ $$ z^{[l+2]} = W^{[l+2]} a^{[l+1]} + b^{[l+2]} $$
Residual path (main path + skip)
$$ a^{[l+2]} = g\!\left(z^{[l+2]} + a^{[l]}\right). $$
  • \(a^{[l]}\) — activation from an earlier layer (the "input" to the block).
  • \(z^{[l+2]}\) — the pre-activation output of the last weight layer in the block.
  • \(a^{[l]} + z^{[l+2]}\) — the skip connection adds the input to the layer output before the final activation.
  • \(g(\cdot)\) — activation function, typically ReLU.
🟡 Important — Same Dimensions Required

For the addition \(z^{[l+2]} + a^{[l]}\) to be valid, both must have the same shape. If the block includes a stride-2 conv (which changes \(n_H, n_W\)) or changes the channel count, you must project \(a^{[l]}\) with a matrix \(W_s\) to match:

$$ a^{[l+2]} = g\!\left(z^{[l+2]} + W_s\,a^{[l]}\right) $$

\(W_s\) is typically a 1×1 convolution with stride matching the main path. In many ResNet blocks, however, all convs use "same" padding and the same channel count, so \(W_s\) is unnecessary (the identity shortcut works directly).

6. Why Do ResNets Work?

Two complementary explanations from Andrew Ng's lecture:

6.1 The "identity is easy" argument

🟢 Intuition

Consider a big network built by stacking residual blocks. If a block's extra layers aren't helping, the block can easily learn to "do nothing" — just set the weights and biases near zero so that \(z^{[l+2]} \approx 0\), and then \(a^{[l+2]} = g(a^{[l]}) = a^{[l]}\) (for ReLU, since activations are non-negative). The block effectively becomes an identity mapping.

$$ a^{[l+2]} = g\!\left(z^{[l+2]} + a^{[l]}\right) $$ $$ \text{If } W^{[l+2]} \approx 0,\; b^{[l+2]} \approx 0 \;\Rightarrow\; z^{[l+2]} \approx 0 \;\Rightarrow\; a^{[l+2]} \approx g(a^{[l]}) = a^{[l]} \text{ (since } a^{[l]} \ge 0\text{)}. $$

This means: adding more layers to a ResNet can only help (or at worst keep performance the same), because the network can always fall back to the identity. That is why very deep ResNets don't suffer the degradation problem that plain networks do.

6.2 The "gradient highway" argument

🟢 Intuition

During backpropagation, gradients must flow from the loss back to early layers. In a plain deep network, the gradient gets multiplied by many weight matrices, causing it to vanish (shrink to zero) or explode. The skip connection provides a direct, unmultiplied path for the gradient to flow — like a "highway" through the network.

6.3 The 34-layer comparison

He et al. compared a 34-layer plain network to a 34-layer ResNet on the same data. The plain network had higher training error, while the ResNet had lower error and trained faster — even though they have the same number of layers.

Figure 6.1 — Same depth (34 layers), but the ResNet adds skip connections every two layers. The skip connections create direct paths for gradients and enable identity mappings.

7. 1×1 Convolutions (Network in Network)

Lin et al., 2013, "Network in network." Introduced the surprisingly powerful idea of using 1×1 filters.

🔵 Definition — 1×1 Convolution

A convolution whose filter has spatial size \(1 \times 1\) but spans all input channels. It does not look at any neighbours — instead it combines information across channels at each spatial position.

🟢 Intuition — What Does It Actually Do?

At each pixel \((i,j)\) you take the vector of \(n_C\) channel values and compute a weighted sum (with bias + ReLU). It's essentially a tiny fully-connected layer applied at every spatial position independently. But because it's implemented as a conv, it fits seamlessly into a CNN.

7.1 Numerical example

Input: a \(6 \times 6 \times 32\) volume. Apply a 1×1 conv with 2 filters. Each filter is \(1\times 1\times 32\). At each of the 36 spatial positions, we compute:

$$ y = \sum_{c=1}^{32} x_c \cdot w_c + b. $$

Where \(x_c\) is the input at channel \(c\) (for that pixel) and \(w_c\) is the filter weight for channel \(c\). One scalar per (filter, pixel). With 2 filters, we get an output of shape \(6\times 6\times 2\) — we just reduced the channel count from 32 to 2.

Figure 7.1 — A 1×1 convolution shrinks (or grows) the number of channels while leaving the spatial dimensions unchanged. Here 32 channels are reduced to 2.

7.2 Where 1×1 convs are used

  • Channel reduction (bottleneck): shrink a large channel dimension before an expensive conv to save compute. This is the key trick inside Inception and ResNet.
  • Channel expansion: use more filters than input channels to grow depth.
  • Adding non-linearity: a 1×1 conv followed by ReLU adds non-linearity across channels without changing spatial size.
🟡 Important — Why Not Just Use a Fully Connected Layer?

A 1×1 conv is essentially a per-pixel FC layer. But as a conv, it:

  • Handles arbitrary spatial sizes without retraining.
  • Adds few parameters (only \(n_C^{in} \times n_C^{out} + n_C^{out}\)).
  • Fits naturally into the convolution pipeline.

8. Inception Network — Motivation

Szegedy et al., 2014, "Going deeper with convolutions." The Inception module asks: why pick a single filter size when you can try several at once?

8.1 The multi-branch idea

Given an input \(28\times28\times192\), run four parallel paths:

  • 1×1 conv, 64 filters → output \(28\times28\times64\)
  • 3×3 conv, 128 filters ("same") → \(28\times28\times128\)
  • 5×5 conv, 32 filters ("same") → \(28\times28\times32\)
  • 3×3 max pool ("same") → \(28\times28\times192\)

Then concatenate along the channel dimension:

$$ 64 + 128 + 32 + 192 = 256 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{output: } 28\times28\times256. $$
Figure 8.1 — The Inception module: parallel branches with different filter sizes, all outputs concatenated along the channel dimension. This lets the network learn multi-scale features simultaneously.

8.2 The computational cost problem

A direct 5×5 "same" conv with 32 filters on a \(28\times28\times192\) input has the cost:

$$ \underbrace{28 \cdot 28 \cdot 32}_{\text{output positions}} \times \underbrace{5 \cdot 5 \cdot 192}_{\text{filter size}} \approx 120 \text{ million multiplications}. $$

That's prohibitively expensive. The fix: use a 1×1 bottleneck first to shrink the channel count.

Figure 8.2 — The bottleneck trick: a 1×1 conv reduces the channel dimension from 192 to 16 before the expensive 5×5 conv. Total cost drops from ~120M to ~12.4M operations (a 10× reduction), with almost no loss in representational power.
🟣 Verify the bottleneck cost

Step 1 — 1×1 conv, 16 filters on \(28\times28\times192\):

$$ (28\cdot 28)\cdot 16 \cdot (1\cdot 1\cdot 192) = 784 \cdot 16 \cdot 192 \approx 2.4\text{M}. $$

Step 2 — 5×5 conv, 32 filters on \(28\times28\times16\):

$$ (28\cdot 28)\cdot 32 \cdot (5\cdot 5\cdot 16) = 784 \cdot 32 \cdot 400 \approx 10.0\text{M}. $$

Total: ≈ 12.4M — about one-tenth the direct cost. ✓

9. The Inception Module (Full Version)

The full Inception module combines the multi-branch idea with the bottleneck trick. Every branch that uses a large filter is preceded by a 1×1 conv that shrinks channels.

Figure 9.1 — The full Inception module. Branches: 1×1 only, 1×1→3×3, 1×1→5×5, and maxpool→1×1. All branches produce \(28\times28\) spatial outputs and are concatenated along channels.
🟡 Important — Why Inception Works
  • Multi-scale features: 1×1, 3×3, 5×5 filters capture different-sized patterns.
  • Cheap: 1×1 bottlenecks cut computation by ~10×.
  • Adaptive: the network learns to weight the branches by how much it uses them.
  • Deep but not too wide: concatenation at the end combines all branches into one tensor.
📎 Additional Context — GoogLeNet

The full Inception network (GoogLeNet) stacks many Inception modules and also includes two auxiliary classifiers attached to intermediate layers to fight vanishing gradients. Modern variants (Inception-v3, v4) refine the module further, but the core "parallel branches + concatenate" idea is the same.

10. MobileNet — Depthwise Separable Convolutions

Howard et al., 2017. Motivation: low computational cost at deployment time, useful for mobile and embedded vision.

🟢 Intuition

A normal convolution does both "spatial filtering" and "channel combination" in one operation. MobileNet splits these two jobs into two cheaper steps:

  1. Depthwise conv: one small filter per input channel (spatial filtering only).
  2. Pointwise conv: a 1×1 conv to combine channels (channel mixing only).

10.1 Normal convolution cost

Example: input \(6\times6\times3\), 5 filters of size \(3\times3\), stride 1, no padding. Output is \(4\times4\times5\).

$$ \text{Cost} = \underbrace{3\cdot 3\cdot 3}_{f\cdot f\cdot n_C} \times \underbrace{4\cdot 4}_{\text{positions}} \times \underbrace{5}_{n_C'} = 2160. $$

10.2 Depthwise separable convolution

Split into two steps:

Step 1 — Depthwise conv. Use \(n_C = 3\) independent \(3\times3\) filters, one for each input channel. Each produces its own feature map. No channel mixing.

$$ \text{Depthwise cost} = \underbrace{3\cdot 3}_{f\cdot f} \times \underbrace{4\cdot 4}_{\text{positions}} \times \underbrace{3}_{n_C} = 432. $$

Step 2 — Pointwise conv. Apply a 1×1 conv with 5 filters to combine the 3 depthwise maps into 5 output channels.

$$ \text{Pointwise cost} = \underbrace{1\cdot 1\cdot 3}_{f\cdot f\cdot n_C} \times \underbrace{4\cdot 4}_{\text{positions}} \times \underbrace{5}_{n_C'} = 240. $$

Total cost: \(432 + 240 = 672\), which is \(672 / 2160 \approx 0.31\) — about one-third the cost of a normal convolution. In real networks the savings are often 8–9× because channel counts are much larger.

Figure 10.1 — Normal conv (top) does spatial filtering and channel mixing together. Depthwise separable (bottom) splits it into depthwise spatial filtering (one filter per channel) + pointwise channel mixing (1×1).
🟡 Important — The Big Win

Depthwise separable convolutions reduce computation by roughly a factor of \(1/n_C' + 1/f^2\). For typical values (\(f=3\), \(n_C'\) large), this is 8–9× cheaper than normal convolution — with minimal accuracy loss.

11. MobileNet v2 — Bottleneck Blocks with Residual Connections

Sandler et al., 2018. MobileNet v2 stacks 17 residual bottleneck blocks instead of plain depthwise-separable layers. Each block has three stages:

  1. Expansion (1×1 conv): expand channels (e.g., 24 → 144).
  2. Depthwise (3×3 conv): spatial filtering at the expanded size.
  3. Projection (1×1 conv): reduce back to a smaller channel count (e.g., 144 → 24).

Plus a residual connection from input to output, applied when shapes match.

Figure 11.1 — MobileNet v2 block. Expand → depthwise → project, with a residual skip. The "bottleneck" name comes from the projection step shrinking channels before the next block.
🟡 Important — Why Expand Then Shrink?

Depthwise convs are only efficient when each channel has enough spatial detail to work with. By expanding first, the depthwise step has more channels to filter. Then projection compresses back, keeping the next block's input small. This trades a little compute for much better accuracy than v1.

MobileNet v2's full architecture: 1 initial 3×3 conv, then 17 bottleneck blocks (with a few downsampling strides), then a final 1×1 conv, global average pool, and a softmax classifier.

12. EfficientNet

Tan & Le, 2019. The question: if you have a compute budget, should you make the network deeper, wider, or feed it higher-resolution images?

🔵 Definition — Compound Scaling

Compound scaling means scaling depth, width, and input resolution together, in a carefully balanced way, rather than one at a time.

Figure 12.1 — Instead of scaling just one axis, EfficientNet scales depth (more layers), width (more channels per layer), and input resolution jointly with a fixed ratio, achieving better accuracy-per-FLOP than scaling any one axis alone.
🟡 Important — The Takeaway

EfficientNet's message: architecture search + principled scaling beats naive scaling. If you have a compute budget, jointly scaling depth, width, and resolution is more efficient than dumping everything into one of them. EfficientNet-B0 through B7 are a family of models with increasing compute/accuracy.

13. Transfer Learning

This is arguably the single most practical lesson of the entire course. Almost nobody trains a big CNN from scratch on a new task — they fine-tune a pretrained network.

🔵 Definition — Transfer Learning

Take a network that was trained on a large dataset (e.g., ImageNet, 1.2M images, 1000 classes), keep its learned weights, and adapt the network to a new task with a much smaller dataset.

13.1 How to apply it — a three-step recipe

  1. Download a pretrained model (e.g., ResNet‑50 trained on ImageNet). Keep its convolutional layers ("the backbone") intact.
  2. Remove the original classification head (the final FC + softmax) and replace it with your own output layer for your task (e.g., softmax over 3 classes).
  3. Choose a training strategy based on dataset size (see table below).
Your datasetStrategyWhy
Very small (e.g., < 1000 examples) Freeze all conv layers; train only the new output layer Too few examples to safely fine-tune the backbone.
Medium (1000–100K) Freeze early layers; fine-tune later layers + new output layer Early layers detect generic edges/textures; later layers can adapt to your domain.
Large (100K+) Fine-tune the entire network (often with a small learning rate) Enough data to safely adapt even the early filters.
🟢 Intuition — Why Does It Work?

The early layers of any CNN learn generic visual features — edges, corners, colour blobs, textures — which are useful for almost any vision task. Later layers learn task-specific features (e.g., "cat ear") which you may need to replace. So transfer learning lets you keep the hard-won generic features and only relearn the last few layers.

🟣 Worked example

You want to classify 500 images of three types of flowers.

  1. Download ResNet‑50 pretrained on ImageNet.
  2. Delete the 1000-unit softmax. Add a new FC layer with 3 outputs + softmax.
  3. Freeze all ResNet layers. Train only the new FC for a few epochs.
  4. Optionally: unfreeze the last residual block and fine-tune with a very small learning rate (e.g., 1e‑4).

This typically achieves strong accuracy with far less data and compute than training from scratch.

🔴 Warning — Two Common Mistakes
  • Fine-tuning with too large a learning rate — destroys the pretrained weights. Use a small LR (often 10× smaller than you'd use for training from scratch).
  • Freezing the whole network and only training the output on a medium dataset — you leave accuracy on the table; unfreeze the later layers.

14. Data Augmentation

More data → better CNNs. When you can't get more images, you can synthesize new ones from your existing data by applying label-preserving transformations.

🔵 Definition — Data Augmentation

Generate additional training examples by applying transformations that preserve the label. The augmented images look different to the network but still show the same class.

14.1 Common augmentation methods

Figure 14.1 — Common augmentations. Mirroring and cropping change geometry; colour shifting changes pixel values. Each produces a "new" training example the network has never seen.

14.2 Color shifting

A technique from AlexNet: add a random offset to each of the R, G, B channels of an image. For example:

$$ (R,G,B) \;\to\; (R + 20,\; G - 20,\; B + 20) $$

Different random offsets give many colour-tinted versions of the same image. The idea is to make the network robust to colour variations (e.g., lighting differences at test time).

📎 Additional Context — PCA Colour Augmentation

AlexNet's original paper also applied PCA-based colour augmentation, which shifts colours along the principal axes of variation in the dataset. This tends to be slightly more effective than pure random offsets but is rarely used in modern pipelines.

14.3 Implementing augmentation during training

🟢 Implementation Pattern

Augmentation is typically applied on the fly during training:

  1. A CPU thread loads a mini-batch of images from disk.
  2. Another thread applies random distortions (mirror, crop, colour shift).
  3. The distorted mini-batch is handed to the GPU for the forward/backward pass.

This keeps the GPU busy while the CPU prepares the next batch.

🔴 Warning — Don't Augment Test Data (Usually)

Augmentation is a training-time technique. The test set should reflect the real distribution you care about. One exception is "test-time augmentation" (TTA) — running the classifier on several augmented versions of a test image and averaging predictions. Used in competitions, less common in production.

15. The State of Computer Vision

Different problems have different amounts of labelled data. Understanding this lets you decide how much hand-engineering (design effort) to invest.

15.1 Data vs hand-engineering

🟢 The Two Sources of Knowledge

Any ML system can draw on:

  • Labelled data \((x, y)\) — the more the better, but expensive.
  • Hand-engineered knowledge — network architecture, loss functions, feature engineering, domain constraints.

When you have little data, you rely more on hand-engineering. When you have lots of data, simpler algorithms with less hand-engineering win.

Figure 15.1 — Different vision tasks sit at different points on this spectrum. Object detection typically has less data (harder labels) → more hand-engineering. Speech recognition has more data → simpler architectures often win.
🟡 Important — Transfer Learning Shifts the Balance

Even when your task has little labelled data, you can borrow a pretrained network — effectively importing the hand-engineering work of others. This is why transfer learning is so powerful for small-data tasks.

15.2 Tips for doing well on benchmarks / winning competitions

These are the techniques Andrew Ng recommends for squeezing out the last few percent of accuracy — mainly useful in research/competition, less so in production:

TechniqueHow it worksWhen to use
Ensembling Train 3–15 independent networks. Average their outputs (or take a vote) at test time. When accuracy matters more than inference cost.
Multi-crop at test time Run the classifier on multiple crops / mirrors of each test image and average the results (e.g., the classic "10-crop"). Competitions; sometimes as a cheap accuracy boost.
Use open-source code Start from published architectures and pretrained models rather than reimplementing from scratch. Almost always.
Use pretrained models Fine-tune an ImageNet-pretrained backbone on your data. Whenever your dataset is small or medium.
🔴 Warning — Ensembling Is Expensive at Inference

If you ensemble 10 networks, you pay 10× the inference cost. In production this is often unacceptable. Use ensembling when accuracy is worth the cost, not by default.

15.3 The classic 10-crop test-time trick

For each test image, extract 10 versions — the four corners, the centre, and their horizontal mirrors — run the classifier on each, and average the softmax outputs. This "votes" against small translations and left/right flips, making the prediction more robust.

16. Weekly Summary

This week surveyed the architectures that made CNNs the dominant tool in vision.

  • LeNet‑5 (1998): CONV-POOL-CONV-POOL-FC-FC-Softmax; ~60K params; established the shrink-spatial / grow-channel pattern.
  • AlexNet (2012): same pattern but much bigger; ReLU + max pooling + multiple GPUs + data augmentation; ~60M params.
  • VGG (2014): radical simplicity — only 3×3 convs; two 3×3 convs have a 5×5 receptive field with fewer params; used as a pretrained backbone.
  • ResNet (2015): residual blocks with skip connections let you train 100+ layer networks. \(a^{[l+2]} = g(z^{[l+2]} + a^{[l]})\).
  • Why ResNets work: the identity shortcut makes it easy for a block to "do nothing," and the shortcut gives gradients a direct path.
  • 1×1 convolutions: operate across channels at every pixel; used to reduce (bottleneck) or expand channels; add non-linearity without changing spatial size.
  • Inception module: run 1×1, 3×3, 5×5 convs and max pool in parallel; concatenate. Use 1×1 bottlenecks to keep compute manageable.
  • MobileNet: depthwise separable convs (depthwise + pointwise) reduce compute ~3–9×.
  • MobileNet v2: bottleneck blocks (expand → depthwise → project) + residual connections.
  • EfficientNet: jointly scale depth, width, and resolution.
  • Transfer learning: fine-tune a pretrained backbone — the single most practical technique in the course.
  • Data augmentation: mirroring, cropping, colour shifting; applied during training, not test.
  • Benchmark tips: ensembling, multi-crop, use open-source code and pretrained models.

\(y = \sum_{c=1}^{n_C} x_c\, w_c + b\) 1×1 convolution: per-pixel weighted sum across channels.
\(\text{Cost}_\text{normal} = f\cdot f\cdot n_C \times n_H' \cdot n_W' \times n_C'\) Compute of a normal convolution (filter size × output positions × filters).
\(\text{Cost}_\text{depthwise} = f\cdot f \times n_H' \cdot n_W' \times n_C\) Depthwise conv cost — one filter per input channel, no channel mixing.
\(\text{Cost}_\text{pointwise} = 1\cdot 1\cdot n_C \times n_H' \cdot n_W' \times n_C'\) Pointwise (1×1) conv cost — combines channels with a 1×1 filter.
\(\text{Cost}_\text{sep} = \text{Cost}_\text{depthwise} + \text{Cost}_\text{pointwise}\) Total cost of a depthwise separable convolution.
\(\dfrac{\text{Cost}_\text{sep}}{\text{Cost}_\text{normal}}\) Typical ratio ≈ \(1/n_C' + 1/f^2\), often 1/8 or better.

18. Common Confusions

Often confusedCorrect distinction
LeNet‑5 vs AlexNet LeNet‑5: small (~60K params), grayscale, sigmoid/tanh, average pooling. AlexNet: 1000× bigger (~60M params), RGB, ReLU, max pooling, multi-GPU.
VGG's "3×3 only" claim VGG uses only 3×3 conv filters (not 11×11 or 5×5 like AlexNet). Its pooling is still 2×2, and it has FC layers at the end.
Residual block vs skip connection The skip connection is the wire that carries \(a^{[l]}\) forward. The residual block is the whole unit (main path + skip + final activation).
Why ResNets don't suffer from degradation Not because they have fewer parameters — they have more. It's because a block can learn the identity mapping, so adding layers can't hurt.
1×1 conv vs FC layer A 1×1 conv is applied per spatial position, so it works on arbitrary image sizes. An FC layer flattens spatial dims, so the input size must be fixed.
Inception module vs Inception network The module is one block (parallel branches + concatenate). The network (GoogLeNet) stacks many modules together.
Depthwise vs pointwise convolution Depthwise: one small filter per input channel (spatial filtering only). Pointwise: 1×1 conv that mixes channels. Together = depthwise separable conv.
Training from scratch vs transfer learning Training from scratch requires a huge dataset and lots of compute. Transfer learning reuses pretrained features and works well even with tiny datasets.
Training-time vs test-time augmentation Training-time augmentation generates new training examples. Test-time augmentation (10-crop, TTA) runs the classifier multiple times per test image and averages.

19. Review Questions

Q1. What is the main architectural difference between LeNet‑5 and AlexNet?
AlexNet is essentially "LeNet but much bigger." It uses ~60M parameters (1000× more than LeNet‑5), a 227×227×3 RGB input (instead of 32×32×1), ReLU activations (instead of sigmoid/tanh), max pooling (instead of average), and was trained across two GPUs on the much larger ImageNet dataset.
Q2. Why does VGG use only 3×3 convolutions?
Because a stack of small filters has the same receptive field as one large filter — two 3×3 convs = 5×5 receptive field, three 3×3 convs = 7×7 — but uses fewer parameters and injects more non-linearity (ReLU after each conv). It's a simpler, more uniform design.
Q3. What problem does ResNet solve, and how?
ResNet solves the degradation problem: plain deep networks get worse training error as depth increases. ResNet's residual blocks add a skip connection so the input \(a^{[l]}\) is added to the output of two layers ahead: \(a^{[l+2]} = g(z^{[l+2]} + a^{[l]})\). This makes it easy to learn the identity mapping, so adding layers can't hurt, and it gives gradients a direct path during backprop.
Q4. Write the equation for a residual block output.
$$a^{[l+2]} = g\!\left(z^{[l+2]} + a^{[l]}\right)$$ where \(z^{[l+2]}\) is the output of the last linear layer in the block and \(a^{[l]}\) is the input via the skip connection.
Q5. What does a 1×1 convolution do?
It performs a per-pixel weighted sum across the channel dimension. It doesn't look at spatial neighbours. Its primary use is to reduce (bottleneck) or increase channel count, adding non-linearity (via ReLU) without changing spatial size.
Q6. How does a 1×1 bottleneck reduce compute in the Inception module?
Instead of running an expensive 5×5 conv directly on a 192-channel input (~120M multiplications), you first run a 1×1 conv with 16 filters to shrink channels to 16, then run the 5×5 conv on the smaller volume (~2.4M + ~10M = ~12.4M multiplications). That's about a 10× reduction with negligible loss in expressiveness.
Q7. Describe the Inception module in one sentence.
It runs 1×1, 3×3, and 5×5 convolutions plus a 3×3 max pool in parallel (each branch is preceded by a 1×1 conv to reduce channels where needed), then concatenates all branch outputs along the channel dimension.
Q8. What is a depthwise separable convolution?
A convolution split into two stages: (1) depthwise — a small filter applied independently to each input channel (spatial filtering only), and (2) pointwise — a 1×1 conv that mixes channels (produces the desired number of output channels). It's much cheaper than a normal convolution.
Q9. Compute the cost of a normal convolution on a 6×6×3 input with a 3×3×3 filter and 5 filters.
$$(3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3) \times (4 \cdot 4) \times 5 = 27 \cdot 16 \cdot 5 = 2160.$$
Q10. Compute the cost of the corresponding depthwise separable convolution.
Depthwise: \(3 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = 432\). Pointwise: \(1 \cdot 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 = 240\). Total = 672. Ratio to normal = 672/2160 ≈ 0.31.
Q11. What are the three stages of a MobileNet v2 bottleneck block?
(1) Expansion — 1×1 conv that expands channels. (2) Depthwise — 3×3 depthwise conv at the expanded size. (3) Projection — 1×1 conv that reduces channels. Plus a residual connection when shapes match.
Q12. What is compound scaling (EfficientNet)?
Scaling a network along three axes jointly: depth (more layers), width (more channels), and input resolution (larger images). EfficientNet finds a good ratio between these and achieves better accuracy per FLOP than scaling any single axis.
Q13. When should you freeze the convolutional backbone and only train the new output layer?
When your target dataset is very small (roughly < 1000 examples). With so little data, fine-tuning the whole network risks overfitting and destroying the pretrained features. Training only the new head is safer.
Q14. What is the difference between training-time augmentation and test-time augmentation?
Training-time augmentation expands your dataset by applying random transformations (mirror, crop, colour shift) to training images. Test-time augmentation runs the classifier on multiple transformed versions of each test image and averages the outputs — often used in competitions ("10-crop").
Q15. What are the "two sources of knowledge" in computer vision?
(1) Labelled data \((x, y)\) — the more you have, the simpler your algorithms can be. (2) Hand-engineered knowledge — network architectures, loss functions, domain-specific design. When data is scarce, you rely more on hand-engineering.
Q16. Why does a 1×1 conv make sense as a bottleneck?
Because spatial convolution cost scales with the number of input channels in the filter. If you shrink channels first with a cheap 1×1 conv, the subsequent expensive spatial conv operates on fewer channels → much less compute. It's essentially dimensionality reduction across channels.
Q17. Why does VGG use so many parameters compared to LeNet‑5?
VGG's parameter count is dominated by its fully connected layers at the end (7×7×512 → 4096, then 4096 → 4096, then 4096 → 1000). These alone contain most of the ~138M parameters. AlexNet has the same pattern (~60M parameters, mostly in FC). In LeNet, everything is small, so total is ~60K.
Q18. What was AlexNet's "Local Response Normalization" and does modern practice still use it?
LRN normalises activations across channels at each position, suppressing very active channels. Modern networks almost never use LRN — batch normalization (from Course 2) has replaced it and works much better.
Q19. Why do modern architectures often replace pooling with strided convolutions?
A strided conv still learns parameters (the filter), so the network can learn how to downsample. Pooling is a fixed operation with no parameters. For accuracy-focused tasks, learning to downsample often wins.
Q20. Give the three practical tips from the "state of computer vision" slide for winning competitions.
(1) Ensembling — train 3–15 networks and average their outputs. (2) Multi-crop at test time — run the classifier on multiple crops/mirrors of each test image and average. (3) Use open-source code and pretrained models — reuse what already works.

20. Self‑Test

Easy

1. Which classic network first used ReLU activations at scale?
AlexNet (2012). LeNet‑5 used sigmoid/tanh.
2. In a residual block, what is added before the final activation?
The input activation \(a^{[l]}\) (via the skip connection) is added to \(z^{[l+2]}\) (the output of the main path), producing \(g(z^{[l+2]} + a^{[l]})\).
3. How many parameters does a 1×1 conv with 32 filters have, on an input with 64 channels (ignore bias)?
\(1 \cdot 1 \cdot 64 \cdot 32 = 2048\).
4. Which is cheaper: a 3×3 conv with 64 filters, or a depthwise separable conv with 3×3 depthwise + 64 pointwise, on 64 input channels?
Normal: \(3\cdot 3 \cdot 64 \cdot 64 = 36{,}864\) per output position. Depthwise: \(9 \cdot 64 = 576\) per position. Pointwise: \(1 \cdot 64 \cdot 64 = 4096\) per position. Total sep = 4672 — roughly 8× cheaper.

Medium

5. Why does the ResNet identity shortcut keep gradients from vanishing?
The skip connection adds \(a^{[l]}\) directly to \(z^{[l+2]}\). During backprop, the gradient from \(a^{[l+2]}\) w.r.t. \(a^{[l]}\) includes a term equal to 1 (from the identity path) plus the usual weight-matrix term. This extra "always-present" gradient provides a direct highway for error signals to flow back, avoiding vanishing gradients in very deep networks.
6. In the Inception module, why is the max-pool branch followed by a 1×1 conv?
Max pooling outputs \(n_C^{in}\) channels (the same as its input). To keep the concatenated output balanced (the other branches produce fewer channels after their 1×1 bottlenecks), a 1×1 conv after the max pool reduces the channel count to a smaller value (e.g., 32). It also adds non-linearity.
7. Why does MobileNet v2 expand channels before the depthwise step?
The depthwise conv has only \(f \cdot f\) weights per channel — very little capacity. Expanding the channels first gives the depthwise step more channels to work with, allowing richer features before the projection step shrinks channels back down.
8. You have 50 images of 5 rare bird species. How would you train a classifier?
Use transfer learning: load a pretrained network (e.g., ResNet‑50 on ImageNet), freeze all its conv layers, replace the classification head with a softmax over 5 classes, and train only the new head. With 50 images (10 per class), fine-tuning the whole network would overfit badly.
9. What is the difference between the Inception module and GoogLeNet?
The Inception module is a single building block (parallel 1×1, 3×3, 5×5 convs + max pool, concatenated). GoogLeNet (Inception v1) is the full network built by stacking many Inception modules together.

Hard

10. Prove that two stacked 3×3 convs have a receptive field equal to one 5×5 conv.
The first 3×3 conv maps an output cell to a 3×3 region of the input. The second 3×3 conv maps its output cell to a 3×3 region of the first conv's output, which corresponds to a 3×3 region of the first conv's inputs (since each first-conv output cell already maps to 3×3 of the original input). The union is a \(3 + (3-1) = 5\) region. So two stacked 3×3 = one 5×5 receptive field. Similarly, three stacked 3×3 = one 7×7.
11. How many parameters does one 5×5 conv with 64 output channels on 64 input channels have? Compare to three stacked 3×3 convs with 64 channels each.
5×5 conv: \(5\cdot 5 \cdot 64 \cdot 64 = 102{,}400\) (plus biases). Three 3×3 convs: \(3 \cdot (3\cdot 3 \cdot 64 \cdot 64) = 3 \cdot 36{,}864 = 110{,}592\). Similar count, but the 3×3 stack has three ReLUs (more non-linearity) and similar receptive field.
12. Derive the depthwise separable cost ratio in terms of \(f\) and \(n_C'\).
Let the number of input channels be \(n_C\) and output channels be \(n_C'\), output spatial size \(H \cdot W\). Normal: \(f^2 \cdot n_C \cdot H \cdot W \cdot n_C'\). Depthwise: \(f^2 \cdot n_C \cdot H \cdot W\). Pointwise: \(1 \cdot n_C \cdot H \cdot W \cdot n_C'\). Total sep: \(H W n_C (f^2 + n_C')\). Ratio: \(\dfrac{f^2 + n_C'}{f^2 n_C'} = \dfrac{1}{n_C'} + \dfrac{1}{f^2}\). For \(f=3, n_C'=64\): ratio = \(1/64 + 1/9 \approx 0.13\) — about 8× cheaper.
13. In a residual block where the main path uses stride 2 (to downsample), why can't you just add \(a^{[l]}\) directly?
Because the shapes no longer match: the main path output \(z^{[l+2]}\) has half the spatial size of \(a^{[l]}\). You need to project \(a^{[l]}\) to match — typically with a 1×1 conv with stride 2 (plus possibly more filters to match channel count), denoted \(W_s\). So \(a^{[l+2]} = g(z^{[l+2]} + W_s a^{[l]})\).
14. What does it mean that MobileNet v2's bottleneck uses "expansion" then "projection"?
The input has few channels (say 24). The expansion 1×1 conv expands it to many channels (say 144). The depthwise conv then operates at this larger channel count, giving it more capacity. The projection 1×1 conv then reduces back to a small channel count (say 24). The input and output both have 24 channels, so a residual connection can be applied — this is the "bottleneck": narrow → wide → narrow.
15. Why does EfficientNet use a fixed scaling coefficient across depth, width, and resolution?
Empirically, scaling one axis alone saturates quickly — you get diminishing returns and the model becomes unbalanced (e.g., very deep but very narrow). Joint scaling with a fixed ratio keeps the three dimensions in proportion, so each helps the others. EfficientNet found this ratio via architecture search and achieved a better accuracy-vs-FLOP Pareto frontier than prior methods.
16. Suppose you want to deploy a classifier on a smartphone with limited compute. Which architecture family from this week is best suited, and why?
MobileNet (v1 or v2) or EfficientNet-Lite. MobileNet's depthwise separable convolutions cut compute by ~3–9× vs a normal conv of the same shape. EfficientNet scales efficiently. Both are designed for mobile/embedded deployment. VGG and ResNet‑152 would be too heavy.
17. In the "data vs hand-engineering" spectrum, why does transfer learning effectively "shift" a small-data task to the right?
Because transfer learning imports the hand-engineering (and learned features) of a network trained on a large dataset. Even though your own task has little data, you get the benefit of the pretrained network's knowledge — so you need less hand-engineering to make it work. Effectively, you've borrowed the "lots of data" advantage.
18. Name three augmentations that AlexNet used and explain briefly why each helps.
(1) Mirroring — horizontal flips double the dataset and make the model invariant to left-right orientation. (2) Random cropping — forces the model to recognise objects even when partially cut off, improving robustness to translation. (3) PCA colour augmentation — varies colour along the dataset's principal colour axes, making the model robust to lighting and colour variations.
19. Explain the intuition behind the classic "10-crop" test-time augmentation.
For each test image, extract the 4 corners and the centre (5 crops), plus their horizontal mirrors (5 more) = 10 versions. Run the classifier on all 10, then average the softmax outputs. This "votes" over small translations and flips, reducing the risk that a single unlucky crop misclassifies the image.
20. In one paragraph, describe how a modern practitioner would build a state-of-the-art image classifier in 2024, borrowing from this week's ideas.
Start from a pretrained backbone (EfficientNet or a modern ResNet variant trained on ImageNet), replace the classification head with a new one matching the target classes, freeze the backbone for a few epochs while training the head, then unfreeze later layers and fine-tune with a small learning rate. Use augmentation (mirror, crop, colour shift) during training. If accuracy is paramount and inference cost is not, ensemble several such models and use test-time augmentation. Use data augmentation to bridge the gap between the (possibly small) target dataset and the pretrained model. This recipe combines LeNet's structural insights, ResNet's depth, Inception's multi-scale intuition, MobileNet/EfficientNet's efficiency, and transfer learning's reuse of pretrained knowledge.
