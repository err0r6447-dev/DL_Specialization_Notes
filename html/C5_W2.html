1. Introduction & Big Picture

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the branch of AI that teaches computers to understand and generate human language — text and speech. Deep learning has transformed NLP because neural networks can learn the meaning of words directly from large amounts of text.

This week answers a deceptively simple question: how do we represent a word to a neural network?

Computers cannot understand the word "orange" — they only understand numbers. So we need a numerical representation of every word in our vocabulary. The naive approach (one-hot vectors) is easy but almost useless because it treats every word as completely unrelated to every other word. The better approach — word embeddings — represents each word as a compact, dense vector of numbers where similar words end up with similar vectors.

🟢 Intuition — The Core Idea of the Week

Imagine you had to describe each word by a list of numbers along "meaning axes": how male/female is it? how royal? how old? how edible? how big? Once every word is described this way, then:

  • Words with similar meaning ("orange" and "apple") will have similar numbers.
  • We can do arithmetic on meaning: king − man + woman ≈ queen.
  • Neural networks can transfer what they learn about "orange" to "apple" because their vectors are close.

That is what word embeddings achieve.

By the end of this week, you will understand:

  1. Why one-hot vectors are a poor representation and what embedding vectors fix.
  2. How to learn embeddings from a large text corpus using Word2Vec and GloVe.
  3. How to use pretrained embeddings in downstream tasks like sentiment classification.
  4. How to detect and reduce harmful bias baked into embeddings.

2. Word Representation

2.1 The Vocabulary

Before we can represent words numerically, we must decide which words exist in our "language." The set of all words we care about is called the vocabulary, usually denoted \(V\).

The course shows a vocabulary like:

$$V = [\,a,\ \text{aaron},\ \dots,\ \text{zulu},\ \langle \text{UNK} \rangle\,]$$

where:

  • Each entry is a distinct word (token).
  • \(\langle \text{UNK} \rangle\) stands for "unknown" — a catch-all token used for any word not in the vocabulary.
  • \(|V| = 10{,}000\) in this example (the size of the vocabulary).
🔵 Definition — Vocabulary (V)

The vocabulary is the finite set of all words (tokens) that our model knows about. Its size \(|V|\) is a hyperparameter we choose. Typical NLP vocabularies range from 10,000 to 1,000,000 words.

🟡 Important — Tokenization

Before building a vocabulary, text must be split into tokens — typically by whitespace and punctuation. Modern systems often use sub-word tokens (e.g., "un", "believ", "able"), but the course for simplicity assumes whole-word tokens.

3. One-Hot Representation

The simplest way to represent a word as a vector is one-hot encoding: a vector that is all zeros except for a single 1 at the position corresponding to that word's index in the vocabulary.

🔵 Definition — One-Hot Vector

If the vocabulary has size \(|V|\), a one-hot vector for the \(i\)-th word is a vector of length \(|V|\) where the \(i\)-th entry is 1 and all other entries are 0.

3.1 Example

Suppose the vocabulary positions are: Man (5391), Woman (9853), King (4914), Queen (7157), Apple (456), Orange (6257). Then:

Figure 1. Each word becomes a column vector of length 10,000 with a single "1" at the word's index in the vocabulary. The red numbers mark the "on" position. Notice the vector for "orange" is almost entirely zeros — it only "knows" where orange sits in the list.

3.2 The Big Problem with One-Hot Vectors

Consider these two sentences:

🟣 Example

Sentence A: "I want a glass of orange juice."
Sentence B: "I want a glass of apple ____."

If a model has learned from Sentence A that "orange juice" is a common pairing, it should be able to guess that Sentence B ends in "juice" — because apple and orange are both fruits.

But if "orange" is \([0,0,\dots,1,\dots,0]\) and "apple" is \([0,\dots,1,\dots,0,0]\) at completely different indices, then their inner product is:

$$\text{orange}^T \cdot \text{apple} = 0$$

The model sees zero similarity between orange and apple. It has no way to know they belong to the same category of fruit.

🔴 Common Mistake

Assuming one-hot vectors carry meaning. They don't. They are just identity labels — like different house numbers on the same street. Two words being "close" in one-hot space means nothing about their meaning.

💡 Insight — What We Need Instead

We need a representation where "orange" and "apple" have vectors that are close together, while "orange" and "king" are far apart. In other words, we need a representation that encodes semantic similarity. This is exactly what word embeddings provide.

4. Featurized Representation: Word Embeddings

🔵 Definition — Word Embedding

A word embedding is a dense, low-dimensional, real-valued vector that represents a word's meaning. Instead of a 10,000-dimensional sparse one-hot vector, each word is mapped to, say, a 300-dimensional dense vector \(\mathbf{e}_w \in \mathbb{R}^{300}\) such that words with similar meanings have vectors that are close in Euclidean (or cosine) distance.

4.1 The Feature-Based Intuition

The clearest mental model is to imagine a set of feature axes. For each word, ask: "how much does this word score on each feature?"

The lecture shows a table like this:

Size………………

Now, instead of a 10,000-length one-hot vector, each word can be represented by the column of these feature values:

$$\mathbf{e}_{\text{Man}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0.01 \\ 0.03 \\ 0.09 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{e}_{\text{Orange}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.01 \\ 0.00 \\ -0.02 \\ 0.97 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}, \quad \dots$$
🟡 Important — Two Ways to Read the Diagram
  • Row view: each row is a "feature" (Gender, Royal, Age, …).
  • Column view: each column is one word's embedding vector \(\mathbf{e}_w\) — a stack of feature scores.

In practice, we never hand-design features. The network learns the embedding vectors from data. The "Gender/Royal/Age/Food" labels are just for human intuition — the actual learned features won't have clean names.

4.2 Why Embeddings Are Useful

Look at the table again:

  • Man and Woman have similar scores on most rows except Gender — they are close in embedding space.
  • King and Queen share Royal = high and differ on Gender.
  • Apple and Orange share Food = high.
  • Man and Apple have essentially nothing in common — they are far apart.
🟢 Intuition — Why This Solves the "Orange vs Apple" Problem

Because \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{apple}}\) and \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{orange}}\) are both high on "Food", "Size", "Cost", "Alive", and low on "Gender", "Royal" — their vector difference is small. So the model that has learned "orange juice" can generalize to "apple juice." This is the entire point of embeddings: they let the model transfer what it learned about one word to semantically similar words.

4.3 Dimensionality

A common choice in the course is a 300-dimensional embedding, though 50, 100, 200, and even 1024 dimensions are used in practice. The vocabulary in the course is often 10,000 words, so the full embedding table has size \(300 \times 10{,}000\).

🔵 Remember

Embedding dimension (\(d_e\)) is a hyperparameter. Bigger vocabularies and more complex tasks often benefit from larger embedding dimensions — but larger dimensions cost more memory and compute.

5. Visualizing Word Embeddings

Embedding vectors live in high-dimensional space (e.g., 300 dimensions), so we can't plot them directly. Instead, we use dimensionality reduction techniques such as t-SNE to squash them down to 2D for human inspection.

🔵 Definition — t-SNE

t-SNE (t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) is an algorithm that maps high-dimensional points into 2D or 3D while trying to keep points that were close in high dimensions close in the low-dimensional plot. It is only a visualization tool — the 2D coordinates are not used for training.

Figure 2. A 2D projection (e.g., t-SNE) of word embeddings. Words used in similar contexts cluster together: royalty and gendered words cluster at the top-left; animals at the top-right; numbers at the bottom-left; fruits at the bottom-right. The exact positions are meaningless — only the relative closeness matters.

🔴 Warning — Don't Over-Read t-SNE Plots

Distances and cluster shapes in t-SNE plots are not fully reliable — t-SNE sometimes creates artificial gaps and clusters. Use them for intuition, not for quantitative analysis.

6. Using Word Embeddings (Transfer Learning)

The single biggest reason embeddings matter in practice is transfer learning: embeddings learned from massive amounts of unlabeled text can be reused to build models for tasks that have very little labeled data.

6.1 A Worked Example — Named Entity Recognition

Task: Given a sentence, identify which words are person names. This is called named entity recognition (NER).

🟣 Example — NER with embeddings

Training data (small):

  • "Sally Johnson is an orange farmer" — Sally Johnson is a person.

Test sentence (never seen before):

  • "Robert Lin is an apple farmer."

If the model uses one-hot vectors, "orange" and "apple" are unrelated. So the model has no reason to think "apple farmer" behaves like "orange farmer."

If the model uses embeddings, "orange" and "apple" have similar vectors. Then recognizing "Sally Johnson" as a person in the training sentence transfers to recognizing "Robert Lin" as a person in the test sentence — because the surrounding context is semantically equivalent.

6.2 The Three-Step Transfer Recipe

  1. Learn embeddings from a large text corpus (1–100 billion words) — or download pretrained embeddings (GloVe, Word2Vec, fastText).
  2. Transfer the embeddings to your new task, which has a much smaller labeled dataset (say 100,000 words). Each word ID is mapped to a 300-dim embedding instead of a 10,000-dim one-hot.
  3. (Optional) Fine-tune the embeddings on the new task's data — if you have enough data, letting the embedding vectors continue to adjust helps them fit the new task's distribution.
⚙️ Implementation — When to Fine-Tune
  • Very small new dataset (e.g., < 10k examples): keep embeddings frozen.
  • Moderate dataset (e.g., 100k+ examples): fine-tune embeddings together with the rest of the model.
  • Huge dataset: you can even learn embeddings from scratch — but starting from pretrained is still cheaper.
💡 Insight — Why This Works

Embeddings encode general-purpose meaning learned from vast text. That meaning is largely task-agnostic — knowing that "orange" and "apple" are similar is useful for NER, sentiment analysis, question answering, translation, and more. Pretraining lets us offload the "what does each word mean?" problem, so we only need to learn the "what does this specific task want?" problem from labels.

7. Relation to Face Encoding

The idea of embedding is not unique to words. In Course 4 (Face Recognition), you learned to encode a face as a 128-dimensional vector. Two encodings are compared with a similarity function: same person → high similarity; different person → low similarity.

Figure 3. The same abstract idea — map a discrete object (a face, a word) to a dense vector so that similarity in vector space = semantic similarity — underlies both face recognition and word embeddings.

💡 Insight — Unified View

Embeddings are a general-purpose machine-learning tool: any time you have discrete entities (users, products, movies, words) that should have a notion of "similarity," you can learn an embedding for them. Word embeddings are the NLP-specific instance.

8. Properties of Word Embeddings — Analogies

One of the most striking properties of good embeddings is that vector arithmetic encodes semantic relationships.

8.1 The Analogy Task

🟣 Example — The Classic Analogy

"Man is to Woman as King is to ____."

The model computes:

$$\mathbf{e}_{\text{man}} - \mathbf{e}_{\text{woman}} \approx \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{e}_{\text{king}} - \mathbf{e}_{\text{queen}} \approx \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}$$

The difference vectors are almost identical — both capture "male → female" along the Gender axis. So to solve the analogy, we look for a word \(w\) whose embedding is closest to:

$$\mathbf{e}_{\text{king}} - \mathbf{e}_{\text{man}} + \mathbf{e}_{\text{woman}} \approx \mathbf{e}_{\text{queen}}$$

Figure 4. Analogies form parallelograms in embedding space. The vector from "man" to "woman" is (approximately) the same as the vector from "king" to "queen": both encode a male→female shift. To solve "man : woman :: king : ?", find the word nearest to \(\mathbf{e}_{\text{king}} - \mathbf{e}_{\text{man}} + \mathbf{e}_{\text{woman}}\).

8.2 More Analogy Examples from the Lecture

AnalogyMeaning relationship
Man : Woman :: Boy : GirlGender
Ottawa : Canada :: Nairobi : KenyaCapital : Country
Big : Bigger :: Tall : TallerComparative degree
Yen : Japan :: Ruble : RussiaCurrency : Country

8.3 The Algorithm for Solving Analogies

  1. Compute the target vector: \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{e}_b - \mathbf{e}_a + \mathbf{e}_c\) for the analogy "a is to b as c is to ?".
  2. Find the word \(w\) in the vocabulary that maximizes similarity with \(\mathbf{v}\):
$$\text{Find } w : \quad \arg\max_w \ \text{sim}\!\left(\mathbf{e}_w,\ \mathbf{e}_b - \mathbf{e}_a + \mathbf{e}_c\right)$$
🟡 Important — Realistic Accuracy

The lecture mentions analogy accuracy around 30–75% depending on the dataset and embeddings. It's impressive but far from perfect — do not assume it always works.

9. Cosine Similarity

To "find the closest word," we need a way to measure how similar two embedding vectors are. The most common measure is cosine similarity.

🔵 Definition — Cosine Similarity

For two vectors \(u, v \in \mathbb{R}^n\),

$$\text{sim}(u, v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\|u\| \, \|v\|}$$

It measures the cosine of the angle between the vectors. It ranges from \(-1\) (opposite directions) through \(0\) (orthogonal) to \(+1\) (identical direction).

9.1 Why Cosine Instead of Euclidean Distance?

  • Cosine ignores vector magnitude — only direction matters. Two words with the same meaning profile but different "strength" (e.g., very royal vs. slightly royal) will still be considered similar.
  • Cosine is naturally bounded in \([-1, 1]\), making it easy to interpret.
  • For embeddings trained with certain objectives, cosine similarity is the standard metric used to evaluate embedding quality.

9.2 Mini Numerical Example

🟣 Example — Cosine Similarity by Hand

Let \(u = [1, 0, 1]\) and \(v = [0, 1, 1]\). Then:

  • \(u \cdot v = 1{\cdot}0 + 0{\cdot}1 + 1{\cdot}1 = 1\)
  • \(\|u\| = \sqrt{1+0+1} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\)
  • \(\|v\| = \sqrt{0+1+1} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\)
  • \(\text{sim}(u, v) = \dfrac{1}{1.414 \times 1.414} = \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.5\)

The angle between them is \(\cos^{-1}(0.5) = 60^\circ\) — moderately similar.

10. Embedding Matrix

Instead of storing 10,000 individual vectors, we store all embeddings in a single embedding matrix \(E\).

🔵 Definition — Embedding Matrix

The embedding matrix \(E\) is a matrix of shape \(d_e \times |V|\), where \(d_e\) is the embedding dimension (e.g., 300) and \(|V|\) is the vocabulary size (e.g., 10,000). The \(j\)-th column of \(E\) is the embedding for the \(j\)-th word in the vocabulary:

$$E \cdot O_j = \mathbf{e}_j$$

where \(O_j\) is the one-hot vector for word \(j\) (a column vector of length \(|V|\) with a 1 at position \(j\)).

Figure 5. Multiplying the embedding matrix \(E\) by a one-hot vector \(O_j\) "selects" the \(j\)-th column of \(E\). This is mathematically elegant, but in practice we just look up the column directly with a specialized embedding-lookup function.

⚙️ Implementation — Lookup > Matrix Multiply

If \(E\) is a \(300 \times 10{,}000\) matrix and \(O_j\) is a 10,000-dim one-hot, then multiplying \(E \cdot O_j\) costs \(300 \times 10{,}000\) floating-point operations — just to pick one column. In real code, we use an embedding lookup instead:

# Pseudocode: embedding lookup in PyTorch / Keras style
e_j = EmbeddingLayer(vocab_size=10000, dim=300)(word_index_j)

# Under the hood: just index column j of E. No matrix multiply needed.

11. Learning Word Embeddings

So far we've assumed embeddings exist. How do we actually learn them? The idea is to train a neural network on a language-modelling task that forces the embeddings to capture word meaning. We start with the historical neural language model of Bengio et al., 2003.

11.1 Neural Language Model — The Setup

Given a sequence of words, predict the next word.

🟣 Example — "I want a glass of orange ___"

Input context: "I want a glass of orange"

Target (label): "juice"

The model must learn to output a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary. Because "juice" is the correct answer, the embeddings for "orange" and "juice" get pulled closer — and also "apple" because apple appears in similar contexts elsewhere in the training corpus.

Figure 6. The neural language model takes a few preceding words as input. Each word is converted to its embedding via \(E\). The embeddings are combined (often averaged or summed) into a hidden representation, then passed through a softmax to predict the next word. The network is trained on a large corpus of text; its side effect is that \(E\) learns meaningful word vectors.

11.2 The Loss

Training uses the standard cross-entropy loss over the vocabulary:

$$\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}, y) = -\sum_{i=1}^{|V|} y_i \log \hat{y}_i$$

where \(y\) is the one-hot true label (1 at the target word) and \(\hat{y}\) is the softmax output (a probability distribution over the vocabulary).

11.3 Key Idea — The "Side Effect"

💡 Insight — Why Language Modelling Learns Embeddings

To predict "juice" after "orange," the model must know that orange is a fruit-like word that is typically followed by juice. The only flexible parameters the network has to encode that knowledge are the embedding vectors. Therefore, backpropagation pushes the embeddings of "orange" and "juice" (and "apple") into positions that reflect their statistical relationships. The embedding matrix \(E\) is where the meaning gets stored.

11.4 Layer Sizes in the Course Example

The lecture notes vocabulary size 10,000, embedding dimension 300, hidden layer 300 (or 1,200 in some versions), and output softmax of size 10,000. The exact sizes vary between implementations — the important point is that E is learned.

12. Context / Target Pairs

If you want to train an embedding matrix, you need to decide what predicts what. There are many possible choices. The lecture lists several:

🟣 Example Sentence

"I want a glass of orange juice to go along with my cereal."

Context typeWhat the model seesTarget
Last 4 words"a glass of orange"juice
4 words on left & right"a glass of orange" + "to go along with"juice
Last 1 word"orange"juice
Nearby 1 word (skip-gram)"orange"juice, glass, my, …
🟡 Important — You Don't Need Labels

Notice that all of these context/target pairs are generated automatically from raw text — no human labeling required. This is why we can use billions of words of unlabeled text to train embeddings.

💡 Insight — Context Choice Affects Embedding Quality
  • Last 4 words: simple, good for small datasets.
  • 4 words left & right: captures more context; better in practice.
  • Last 1 word: too little context.
  • Nearby 1 word (skip-gram): extremely simple and works remarkably well — this is the basis of Word2Vec.

13. Word2Vec — Skip-Grams

Word2Vec is a family of embedding-learning algorithms introduced by Mikolov et al. at Google. The two main variants are:

  • Skip-gram: given a context word, predict nearby target words.
  • CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words): given surrounding context words, predict the center word.

The course focuses on skip-grams and negative sampling.

13.1 Skip-Gram Intuition

🟢 Intuition — Words That Share Contexts Share Meaning

The skip-gram algorithm says: "If two words tend to appear near the same kinds of words, they probably mean similar things." So instead of predicting whether "orange" and "apple" are similar directly, we train the model to predict whether they co-occur with the same neighbors. That indirect task produces embeddings that capture meaning.

13.2 Skip-Gram Model Formulation

Take the sentence:

"I want a glass of orange juice to go along with my cereal."

Fix "orange" as the context word \(c\). The skip-gram model tries to predict each nearby word \(t\) (the target):

Context \(c\)Target \(t\)
orangejuice
orangeglass
orangemy
orange(other nearby words)

13.3 The Model Equation

We start by mapping the context word to its embedding:

$$\mathbf{e}_c = E \cdot O_c$$

where \(O_c\) is the one-hot vector of the context word and \(E\) is the embedding matrix. Then we compute a softmax over the whole vocabulary:

$$p(t \mid c) = \frac{\exp\!\left(\theta_t^T \mathbf{e}_c\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \exp\!\left(\theta_j^T \mathbf{e}_c\right)}$$

where:

  • \(c\) = context word (input).
  • \(t\) = target word (the word we are trying to predict).
  • \(\mathbf{e}_c \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e}\) = embedding of the context word.
  • \(\theta_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e}\) = a separate output parameter vector associated with target word \(t\).
  • \(|V|\) = vocabulary size.
  • The denominator normalizes over all words so the output is a valid probability distribution.
🟡 Important — Two Sets of Vectors

In skip-gram there are two vector sets: the input embeddings \(\mathbf{e}_w\) (columns of \(E\)) and the output parameters \(\theta_w\). Both are learned. At the end, the input embeddings are usually kept as the word vectors.

13.4 The Loss Function

We train the model with cross-entropy loss:

$$\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}, y) = -\sum_{i=1}^{|V|} y_i \log \hat{y}_i$$

where \(y\) is the one-hot true target word and \(\hat{y}\) is the softmax output.

13.5 The Problem with Plain Softmax

🔴 Warning — The Softmax Is Expensive

Computing the denominator \(\sum_{j=1}^{10{,}000} \exp(\theta_j^T \mathbf{e}_c)\) requires summing over the entire vocabulary for every training example. If the vocabulary is 1,000,000 words (realistic for modern NLP), each step is prohibitively slow. This is the main practical bottleneck of skip-gram with softmax.

14. Problems with Softmax Classification — Hierarchical Softmax

One solution to the expensive-softmax problem is hierarchical softmax. Instead of a flat 10,000-way classification, we organize the vocabulary as a binary tree.

Figure 7. A hierarchical softmax tree. To assign probability to a word, the model follows a path from the root to that word's leaf, making a binary decision at each node. The cost is \(O(\log |V|)\) rather than \(O(|V|)\) per example.

The lecture notes two important consequences:

  • Instead of \(|V|\) probabilities to compute, we only need \(\log_2 |V|\) binary decisions along the path.
  • Frequent words end up near the top of the tree; rare words sink deeper. The tree is usually built using Huffman coding so that frequent words get shorter paths.
🟡 Important — This Doesn't Fully Solve It

Hierarchical softmax is faster than flat softmax but still complex to implement. A simpler and often preferred alternative is negative sampling, which we cover next.

14.1 How to Sample the Context \(c\)?

Another subtlety: if we sample context words uniformly from the corpus, we will be swamped by common words like "the," "of," "a," "and," "to" — which carry little semantic information. The lecture suggests balancing:

  • Sampling more from frequent words so the model learns useful frequent-word associations.
  • Sampling some from less frequent words so the model still learns about rare words.
🟣 Example — Skewed Sampling

Sample 1% from common words (the, of, a, and, to), 99% from less common words (orange, apple, division). This is a heuristic that balances coverage and signal.

15. Negative Sampling

Negative sampling is a clever reformulation that avoids the expensive softmax entirely. Instead of a 10,000-way classification, we turn the problem into 10,000 tiny binary classifications.

15.1 The New Learning Problem

Given a context word \(c\), we generate:

  • 1 positive example: a real target word that actually occurred near \(c\), labelled \(y = 1\).
  • \(k\) negative examples: random words drawn from the vocabulary that did not occur near \(c\), labelled \(y = 0\).
🟣 Example — Positive and Negative Pairs

Context = "orange":

ContextWordTarget \(y\)
orangejuice1
orangeking0
orangebook0
orangethe0
orangeof0

15.2 Choosing \(k\)

Dataset sizeTypical \(k\)
Small dataset5 – 20
Large dataset2 – 5

Smaller \(k\) is used when data is abundant (each example is cheap to generate); larger \(k\) compensates when data is scarce.

15.3 The Binary Classification Model

For each (context, word) pair, the model outputs:

$$P(y=1 \mid c, t) = \sigma\!\left(\theta_t^T \mathbf{e}_c\right)$$

where:

  • \(c\) = context word.
  • \(t\) = candidate target word.
  • \(\mathbf{e}_c\) = embedding of the context word (column of \(E\)).
  • \(\theta_t\) = output parameter vector for candidate word \(t\).
  • \(\sigma\) = sigmoid function \(\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\).

The training objective for a single pair is the standard binary cross-entropy:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\left[\, y \log \hat{y} + (1-y) \log(1-\hat{y}) \,\right]$$

15.4 How to Sample Negative Words

Negative words are sampled from the vocabulary according to a distribution based on word frequencies:

$$P(w_i) = \frac{f(w_i)^{3/4}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} f(w_j)^{3/4}}$$

where \(f(w_i)\) is the empirical frequency of word \(w_i\) in the corpus.

🟡 Important — Why the 3/4 Exponent?

If we sample proportional to raw frequency \(f(w_i)\), common words like "the" dominate. If we sample uniformly (\(1/|V|\)), rare words are over-sampled. The \(3/4\) power is a heuristic in between: it dampens the dominance of very frequent words while still giving them more weight than rare words. This was found empirically to work well in the original Word2Vec paper.

15.5 Why This Is Fast

💡 Insight — From |V| to k+1

Instead of computing a normalization over 10,000 outputs, we do \(k+1\) sigmoid evaluations (each a single dot product + sigmoid) per training example. If \(k = 5\), that's 6 evaluations instead of 10,000 — roughly 1000× faster. This is why negative sampling made it feasible to train embeddings on billions of words.

🟣 Example — Same Corpus, Different Learning Problem

Original softmax problem: "Given context 'orange', what is the distribution over 10,000 words?"

Negative sampling problem: "Given pair (orange, juice), is it real? Given pair (orange, king), is it real? …" — \(k+1\) binary questions.

16. GloVe Word Vectors

GloVe (Global Vectors for Word Representation) is another popular embedding-learning method, developed by Pennington, Socher, and Manning at Stanford. It is simpler in some ways than Word2Vec and leverages global co-occurrence statistics of the corpus.

🔵 Definition — GloVe

GloVe learns word vectors by factorizing a word-context co-occurrence matrix \(X\), where \(X_{ij}\) counts how often word \(j\) appears in the context of word \(i\). The learned vectors are optimized so that their dot product predicts \(\log X_{ij}\).

16.1 The Co-Occurrence Count \(X_{ij}\)

$$X_{ij} = \#\{\text{times word } i \text{ appears in context of word } j\}$$

The context is usually defined as a window of a few words around \(j\).

GloVe is symmetric in an important sense:

$$X_{ij} = X_{ji}$$

(If you use a symmetric context window — e.g., 10 words on either side.) This symmetry is what makes the resulting vectors commute nicely under swap.

16.2 The GloVe Objective

The training objective is to minimize:

$$\min \sum_{i=1}^{|V|} \sum_{j=1}^{|V|} f(X_{ij}) \left( \theta_i^T \mathbf{e}_j + b_i + b_j' - \log X_{ij} \right)^2$$

where:

  • \(\theta_i\) = output vector for word \(i\).
  • \(\mathbf{e}_j\) = input embedding for word \(j\).
  • \(b_i, b_j'\) = bias terms for \(i\) and \(j\).
  • \(X_{ij}\) = co-occurrence count between \(i\) and \(j\).
  • \(f(X_{ij})\) = a weighting function that down-weights very frequent pairs and zeros out pairs that never co-occur: \(f(0) = 0\).
🟡 Important — Why the Weighting Function \(f\)?
  • When \(X_{ij} = 0\), the log is undefined. Setting \(f(0) = 0\) removes those terms.
  • Very frequent pairs (e.g., "the of") would otherwise dominate. Weighting function \(f\) clamps their contribution.
  • The exact form used in the paper is \(f(x) = \min(1, (x/x_{\max})^{0.75})\) with \(x_{\max} = 100\).

16.3 The Final Word Vector

Because GloVe is symmetric in \(i\) and \(j\), the two vectors \(\theta_i\) and \(\mathbf{e}_i\) are essentially interchangeable. In practice, the final embedding for word \(w\) is the average:

$$\mathbf{e}_w^{\text{(final)}} = \frac{\mathbf{e}_w + \theta_w}{2}$$

16.4 What Makes GloVe Distinct

AspectWord2Vec (Skip-Gram)GloVe
ApproachIterates over contexts; predicts targetsFactorizes a global co-occurrence matrix
Uses global statisticsImplicitly, via many passesExplicitly, via \(X_{ij}\)
SpeedFast with negative samplingFast because it does fewer passes over the corpus
QualityExcellentComparable / sometimes better on analogy tasks
Key ideaPredict local contextRegress \(\log X_{ij}\) from vector dot products
💡 Insight — All Methods Are "The Same Family"

Word2Vec, GloVe, and others ultimately all learn embeddings by solving some optimization problem whose solution pushes \(\mathbf{e}_i \cdot \mathbf{e}_j\) to be large when words \(i\) and \(j\) co-occur frequently. The differences are mostly in how the objective is formulated and how efficiently it can be optimized.

17. Sentiment Classification

Sentiment classification is the task of deciding whether a piece of text expresses a positive or negative opinion. It's one of the most common NLP applications and a great way to see embeddings in action.

🟣 Example — Restaurant Reviews
  • "The dessert is excellent." → ★★★★
  • "Service was quite slow." → ★★
  • "Good for a quick meal, but nothing special." → ★★★
  • "Completely lacking in good taste, good service, and good ambience." → ★
🟡 Important — Why This Is Hard

The training set may be small (~100,000 words). Without pretrained embeddings, the model would need to learn word meanings from scratch — impossible with so little data. With embeddings, semantic knowledge transfers from the pretraining corpus.

17.1 A Simple Averaging Model

The simplest sentiment classifier:

  1. Convert each word in the review to its embedding \(\mathbf{e}_w\).
  2. Average all embeddings into a single vector.
  3. Feed the average into a softmax that outputs a star rating (1–5).

Figure 8. A simple sentiment classifier averages the embeddings of all words in the review and feeds the result into a softmax. It's fast and uses embedding knowledge, but ignores word order.

17.2 The Problem with Averaging

🔴 Warning — Averaging Ignores Word Order

Consider: "Completely lacking in good taste, good service, and good ambience."

Because "good" appears three times, the average embedding is heavily "good" → the classifier may wrongly label this review as positive, even though "lacking in good" is obviously negative.

Averaging cannot represent negation or word order. To fix this, we need a sequence model — an RNN.

18. RNN for Sentiment Classification

Instead of averaging, we feed the word embeddings into a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN). The RNN reads the words one by one and builds up a representation that respects order.

Figure 9. Many-to-one RNN for sentiment classification. Each word's embedding \(e\) is fed into an RNN cell. The hidden state \(a^{\langle t\rangle}\) is passed along, capturing the meaning of the sequence so far. The final hidden state \(a^{\langle 10\rangle}\) is fed through a softmax to produce the sentiment probability \(\hat{y}\).

18.1 Why the RNN Works Better

  • It processes words in order, so "lacking in good" is not equivalent to "good."
  • The hidden state \(a^{\langle t\rangle}\) acts as a summary of everything seen so far.
  • If pretrained embeddings are used, the RNN only needs to learn the sentiment task on top of them — much less data required.
💡 Insight — Course 5 Preview

The next weeks of Course 5 (Weeks 3–4) will develop RNNs, GRUs, LSTMs, and attention in much more detail. Here, the point is that embeddings + sequence models together form the backbone of modern NLP.

19. The Problem of Bias in Word Embeddings

Embeddings are learned from real-world text, which contains real-world stereotypes. As a result, embeddings often reproduce and amplify those stereotypes.

🔴 Warning — Embeddings Reflect Human Bias

The lecture gives these examples:

  • Man : Woman  ::  Computer Programmer : Homemaker
  • Father : Doctor  ::  Mother : Nurse

These analogies are true in the embedding space because they are true (statistically) in the training corpus. The model isn't inventing bias — it is learning it from human text.

🟡 Important — Types of Bias

Embeddings can reflect bias along many axes: gender, ethnicity, age, sexual orientation, religion, nationality, and more. Removing bias is an active research area — the methods below are a starting point, not a complete solution.

20. Debiasing Word Embeddings

There are three main steps to reduce (not eliminate) gender bias in an embedding space. The lecture is careful to note that "eliminate" is too strong a word — bias is often entangled with legitimate meaning.

Step 1 — Identify the Bias Direction

We compute the difference between pairs of gendered words and average them to get a single bias direction in the embedding space:

$$\text{bias direction} \approx \text{mean}\!\left( \mathbf{e}_{\text{he}} - \mathbf{e}_{\text{she}},\ \mathbf{e}_{\text{male}} - \mathbf{e}_{\text{female}},\ \dots \right)$$

Concretely, you take many gender-defining pairs (he/she, male/female, boy/girl, …), compute each difference vector, and average them. The result is a single direction \(g \in \mathbb{R}^{300}\).

🟡 Important — Why "Non-Bias Direction" Is 299D

If the embedding space has 300 dimensions and the bias direction is one direction in that space, then the subspace orthogonal to it has 299 dimensions. The lecture calls this the "non-bias direction (299D)". Neutralizing a word means projecting it onto that 299-dimensional subspace, effectively removing its component along the bias direction.

Step 2 — Neutralize Non-Definitional Words

Many words have no inherent gender — "doctor," "babysitter," "engineer," "teacher." We project each such word's embedding onto the non-bias direction, so its component along the bias axis is removed.

Figure 10. Neutralization projects each non-definitional word onto the 299-dimensional subspace orthogonal to the bias direction. The word loses its component along the gender axis but retains all other meaning. Words like "grandmother" and "grandfather" are not neutralized — they are genuinely gendered.

Step 3 — Equalize Pairs

Some word pairs should differ only along the gender axis — e.g., "grandmother"/"grandfather," "girl"/"boy." After neutralization, we still want these pairs to be symmetric around the bias axis so that neither side is favored.

🟣 Example — Equalizing Grandmother & Grandfather

Compute the axis perpendicular to the bias direction, and make the two words equally distant from the bias axis:

  • Before equalization: "grandmother" might be slightly closer to "babysitter" than "grandfather" is to "babysitter."
  • After equalization: grandmother and grandfather are symmetric with respect to the bias axis, so neither is closer to gender-stereotyped words.
🔴 Warning — Debiasing Is Not Perfect
  • It only removes linear bias directions. Non-linear bias can remain.
  • Bias often co-occurs with legitimate meaning (e.g., "mother" really is gendered). Aggressive neutralization can erase meaningful distinctions.
  • Bias can be reintroduced by downstream fine-tuning.

Debiasing is an important tool, but treating it as a complete solution would be a mistake.

💡 Insight — Why This Matters

If an embedding is used in a hiring tool, a loan-approval system, or a translation product, the bias in the embedding can translate into unfair outcomes for real people. Debiasing embeddings is one of the earliest places where responsible AI becomes concrete.

21. Weekly Summary

This week built a complete pipeline: from raw words → one-hot vectors → word embeddings → applications → ethical considerations.

  • One-hot vectors are simple but useless for capturing meaning — every word is orthogonal to every other.
  • Word embeddings map each word to a dense vector where similar words have similar vectors.
  • Embeddings are usually learned from a large unlabeled corpus, then transferred to a downstream task with little labeled data.
  • Embeddings exhibit remarkable properties: analogies (king − man + woman ≈ queen), clustering of similar words, and cosine similarity that behaves sensibly.
  • We store all embeddings in an embedding matrix \(E \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e \times |V|}\) and look up the \(j\)-th column via \(E \cdot O_j\).
  • Learning methods: neural language model (Bengio et al.), Word2Vec skip-gram with negative sampling, and GloVe which factorizes co-occurrence counts.
  • For sentiment classification, a simple averaging model works but ignores word order; an RNN fixes this.
  • Embeddings carry bias from their training corpus. Debiasing involves (1) identifying the bias direction, (2) neutralizing non-definitional words, (3) equalizing genuinely gendered pairs.

23. Important Equations (Reference)

🔵 1. Embedding Lookup
$$E \cdot O_j = \mathbf{e}_j$$

\(E\) is the embedding matrix of shape \(d_e \times |V|\), \(O_j\) is the one-hot vector for word \(j\), and \(\mathbf{e}_j\) is the resulting \(d_e\)-dimensional embedding.

🔵 2. Cosine Similarity
$$\text{sim}(u, v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\|u\| \, \|v\|}$$

Measures the angle between two vectors. Range \([-1, 1]\).

🔵 3. Analogy Solution
$$\arg\max_w \ \text{sim}\!\left(\mathbf{e}_w,\ \mathbf{e}_{\text{king}} - \mathbf{e}_{\text{man}} + \mathbf{e}_{\text{woman}}\right)$$

Finds the word whose embedding best matches the "gender-shifted king" direction.

🔵 4. Skip-Gram Softmax
$$p(t \mid c) = \frac{\exp(\theta_t^T \mathbf{e}_c)}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \exp(\theta_j^T \mathbf{e}_c)}$$

Probability of target \(t\) given context \(c\). \(\theta_t\) = output parameter for word \(t\); \(\mathbf{e}_c\) = embedding of context \(c\).

🔵 5. Cross-Entropy Loss (Language Model)
$$\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}, y) = -\sum_{i=1}^{|V|} y_i \log \hat{y}_i$$

\(y\) = one-hot true label; \(\hat{y}\) = predicted probability distribution.

🔵 6. Negative Sampling Probability
$$P(y=1 \mid c, t) = \sigma\!\left(\theta_t^T \mathbf{e}_c\right), \quad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$

Binary classifier for a (context, word) pair. Trained with binary cross-entropy.

🔵 7. Negative Sampling Distribution
$$P(w_i) = \frac{f(w_i)^{3/4}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} f(w_j)^{3/4}}$$

Distribution for drawing negative examples. The \(3/4\) power dampens the dominance of very frequent words.

🔵 8. GloVe Objective
$$\min \sum_{i,j=1}^{|V|} f(X_{ij}) \left( \theta_i^T \mathbf{e}_j + b_i + b_j' - \log X_{ij} \right)^2$$

Regresses the log co-occurrence \(\log X_{ij}\) from the dot product of two word vectors plus biases.

🔵 9. GloVe Final Embedding
$$\mathbf{e}_w^{\text{(final)}} = \frac{\mathbf{e}_w + \theta_w}{2}$$

Averages the two symmetric representations to get the final word vector.

🔵 10. Bias Direction
$$\text{bias} = \text{mean}\!\left( \mathbf{e}_{\text{he}} - \mathbf{e}_{\text{she}},\ \mathbf{e}_{\text{male}} - \mathbf{e}_{\text{female}},\ \dots \right)$$

Average of many gender-pair difference vectors gives the direction of gender bias in the embedding space.

24. Common Confusions

Concept AConcept BKey difference

Input embedding \(\mathbf{e}_w\) Output parameter \(\theta_w\) Both are vectors for the same word, but one is used as input and the other as output in skip-gram. GloVe averages them at the end.
Word2Vec (skip-gram) GloVe Word2Vec: predicts context/target pairs (local). GloVe: factorizes global co-occurrence counts \(X_{ij}\).
Hierarchical softmax Negative sampling Both speed up training. Hierarchical: \(O(\log |V|)\) via a tree. Negative sampling: turns 1 softmax into \(k+1\) binary problems.

25. Quick Review (Q&A)

Click each question to reveal its answer.

Q1. Why is one-hot representation bad for NLP?

Because it treats every word as completely unrelated to every other word — the inner product of any two different one-hot vectors is 0. There is no notion of similarity, so a model cannot generalize what it learns about "orange" to "apple."

Q2. What is a word embedding, in one sentence?

A dense, low-dimensional vector representation of a word such that words with similar meanings end up with similar vectors.

Q3. What is the shape of the embedding matrix \(E\)?

\(d_e \times |V|\) where \(d_e\) is the embedding dimension (e.g., 300) and \(|V|\) is the vocabulary size (e.g., 10,000). Each column is one word's embedding.

Q4. What does \(E \cdot O_j\) compute?

It selects the \(j\)-th column of \(E\) — i.e., the embedding for word \(j\). Multiplying by a one-hot vector is equivalent to a lookup.

Q5. Why do we use an embedding lookup instead of matrix multiply in practice?

Because \(E \cdot O_j\) wastes computation — it multiplies by thousands of zeros. A direct lookup of column \(j\) is far faster and produces the same result.

Q6. What is the skip-gram model trying to predict?

Given a context word \(c\) ("orange"), predict each nearby target word \(t\) ("juice", "glass", "my", …) that occurs within a window around \(c\).

Q7. Why is softmax over the full vocabulary expensive in skip-gram?

The denominator \(\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \exp(\theta_j^T \mathbf{e}_c)\) sums over the entire vocabulary for every training example. For \(|V| = 10^6\), that's millions of operations per step.

Q8. How does hierarchical softmax reduce this cost?

It organizes the vocabulary as a binary tree. Each word corresponds to a path from root to leaf, and the model makes \(\log_2 |V|\) binary decisions instead of computing \(|V|\) probabilities.

Q9. What problem does negative sampling solve, and how?

It replaces the expensive \(|V|\)-way softmax with \(k+1\) binary classification problems: 1 positive (real context-target pair) and \(k\) negative (randomly drawn) pairs. Each is a simple sigmoid on a dot product. This is ~1000× faster when \(k \ll |V|\).

Q10. What is the difference between \(P(w_i) = 1/|V|\) and \(P(w_i) \propto f(w_i)^{3/4}\) for negative sampling?

Uniform sampling (\(1/|V|\)) over-samples rare words. Raw frequency over-samples common words like "the". The \(3/4\) power is a middle ground — it dampens the dominance of frequent words while still giving them more weight than rare words.

Q11. What is the GloVe objective minimizing?

\(\sum_{i,j} f(X_{ij}) \left( \theta_i^T \mathbf{e}_j + b_i + b_j' - \log X_{ij} \right)^2\) — it tries to make the dot product of two word vectors (plus biases) predict the log of their co-occurrence count.

Q12. Why does the GloVe weighting function \(f\) have \(f(0) = 0\)?

Because if \(X_{ij} = 0\), \(\log X_{ij}\) is undefined. Setting \(f(0) = 0\) removes those terms from the loss, avoiding the problem and also preventing the objective from being dominated by the enormous number of non-co-occurring pairs.

Q13. Why is cosine similarity preferred over Euclidean distance for embeddings?

Because cosine ignores magnitude and depends only on direction. Two words that are semantically similar but differ in "intensity" still get a high cosine similarity. Cosine is also naturally bounded in \([-1, 1]\), making it easy to interpret.

Q14. How would you solve "Man : Woman :: King : ?" using embeddings?

Compute \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{e}_{\text{king}} - \mathbf{e}_{\text{man}} + \mathbf{e}_{\text{woman}}\) and find the word \(w\) that maximizes \(\text{sim}(\mathbf{e}_w, \mathbf{v})\). Answer: "queen".

Q15. Why is a simple averaging classifier bad for sentiment classification?

It ignores word order. "Good for a quick meal, but nothing special" and "nothing good, special for a quick meal" have identical averages, even though they could have different sentiments. Also, "lacking in good" gets a strong positive contribution from "good" alone.

Q16. How does an RNN fix the averaging problem?

It processes words sequentially and maintains a hidden state that captures the order. So "lacking in good" is processed differently from "good, lacking in." Negation and word order can be modeled.

Q17. What are the three steps to debias word embeddings?

(1) Identify the bias direction by averaging gender-pair difference vectors. (2) Neutralize non-definitional words by projecting them onto the 299-D subspace orthogonal to the bias direction. (3) Equalize genuinely gendered pairs (grandmother/grandfather) so neither is closer to gender-stereotyped words.

Q18. Why can't debiasing fully remove bias?

It only removes linear bias directions; non-linear bias can remain. Bias is often entangled with legitimate meaning (mother is gendered by definition), and downstream fine-tuning can reintroduce bias. Debiasing is a mitigation, not a cure.

Q19. What is the difference between the input embedding \(\mathbf{e}_w\) and the output parameter \(\theta_w\) in Word2Vec?

They are both \(d_e\)-dimensional vectors for the same word, but they are learned separately: \(\mathbf{e}_w\) is used when the word is the input (context), \(\theta_w\) is used when it is the output (target) in the softmax. In GloVe, both are learned and averaged to get the final embedding.

Q20. What is the core reason word embeddings enable transfer learning?

Because embeddings encode general-purpose semantics learned from a huge unlabeled corpus. A downstream task with little labeled data can reuse these semantics directly, only learning the task-specific head on top — rather than learning word meaning from scratch.

ST1. In your own words, what is the difference between one-hot and embedding representations?

One-hot is a sparse vector of length \(|V|\) with a single 1 — it says nothing about similarity between words. An embedding is a dense vector of length \(d_e\) (much smaller than \(|V|\)) whose direction encodes meaning, so similar words get similar vectors.

ST2. What is the vocabulary size in the course's running example, and what is the embedding dimension?

ST3. Give two examples of analogy tasks that embeddings can solve.

Any two from: Man:Woman :: Boy:Girl; Ottawa:Canada :: Nairobi:Kenya; Big:Bigger :: Tall:Taller; Yen:Japan :: Ruble:Russia; Man:Woman :: King:Queen.

Medium

ST4. Why does the course call embeddings a "side effect" of the language model training?

Because the training objective of the language model is to predict the next word, not to learn meaningful vectors. But the only flexible place to store word meaning is the embedding matrix, so learning to predict the next word forces the embeddings to become meaningful as a byproduct.

ST5. Explain why the negative sampling objective is essentially \(10{,}000\) binary classification problems instead of one \(10{,}000\)-way classification problem.

Instead of asking "which of 10,000 words is most likely?", negative sampling asks "is this particular (context, word) pair real or fake?" — a yes/no question. Each training example creates 1 positive + \(k\) negative binary pairs, each solved by a sigmoid. The class of "all words" is decomposed into individual binary decisions.

ST6. Suppose you have a small labeled dataset of 5,000 movie reviews. Should you freeze or fine-tune the pretrained embeddings? Justify.

With only 5,000 reviews, you should freeze the embeddings. The task-specific signal is weak, so fine-tuning would overfit and damage the general semantic knowledge. Fine-tuning is typically reserved for larger datasets (100,000+ examples).

ST7. Why does the GloVe objective include two bias terms \(b_i\) and \(b_j'\) instead of just one?

Because GloVe treats \(i\) (the input word) and \(j\) (the context word) as having separate roles, so each gets its own bias. The symmetry between the two is handled later by averaging \(\mathbf{e}_w\) and \(\theta_w\) into a final embedding.

Hard

ST8. The lecture mentions that embeddings can encode bias. Explain a mechanism by which a model could amplify bias relative to the training corpus.

Because embedding training optimizes for statistical prediction — it maximizes the probability of co-occurring pairs. Even if a biased association appears only slightly more often in text (e.g., "programmer" is 60% male), the loss gradient will push the embeddings toward that pattern, and the resulting geometry (e.g., the direction of "male→female") can concentrate this bias into a single axis, making it look larger in the embedding space than it looks in raw statistics. Additionally, downstream models using these embeddings can inherit and apply the amplified signal.

ST9. You want to solve analogies using cosine similarity. What could go wrong if you don't normalize embeddings first?

Cosine similarity already normalizes by \(\|u\| \|v\|\), so it is invariant to magnitude. So the question is really: what could go wrong if you used raw dot products instead of cosine? Raw dot products would be biased toward high-magnitude vectors — words with larger embeddings would tend to win arguments even if their direction is wrong. Normalizing (i.e., using cosine) removes this magnitude bias.

ST10. You're told that the training corpus for a GloVe model contains 1 billion words and \(|V| = 500{,}000\). Estimate the memory footprint of the co-occurrence matrix \(X\), and explain why the weighting function \(f\) is essential.

\(X\) has shape \(|V| \times |V| = 500{,}000 \times 500{,}000 = 2.5 \times 10^{11}\) entries. If stored as 4-byte floats, that's ~1 terabyte. Most entries are zero (since any word pair only co-occurs sometimes). The weighting function \(f\) sets \(f(0) = 0\), so the vast majority of zero entries contribute nothing to the loss. Without \(f\), the loss would be undefined for \(X_{ij} = 0\) (log 0) and dominated by zeros. GloVe implementations exploit sparsity to store only nonzero \(X_{ij}\).

ST11. Why might averaging the two GloVe vectors \(\mathbf{e}_w\) and \(\theta_w\) give a better final embedding than using just \(\mathbf{e}_w\)?

Because GloVe is symmetric: word \(i\) appears as input in some pairs and as context in others. \(\mathbf{e}_w\) captures the "input role" and \(\theta_w\) captures the "context role." Both contain the same underlying meaning, but each is noisier on its own. Averaging reduces the variance and yields a more stable, symmetric final representation.

ST12. In the sentiment classification RNN, why does the lecture say it works even if the RNN does not use pretrained embeddings? And why do pretrained embeddings still help?

An RNN can in principle learn embeddings from scratch on the sentiment task because it can backpropagate through the embedding layer. But with only ~100k words of training data, learning word meanings from scratch is difficult. Pretrained embeddings provide a strong initialization — the RNN only needs to learn how sentiment relates to those already-meaningful vectors. This is the same transfer-learning argument as elsewhere in the week.
